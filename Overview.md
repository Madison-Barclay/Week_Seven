Archaeological networks, they are a great tool in theory, but when you start to use them, pick them apart, build one yourself, things become a bit more nuanced and complex.
The biggest challenge when working with or building an archaeological network, is interpretation - there are many ways to interpret a dataset. In class we dealt with a list of family names, from a cemetery plot. There's only so much information available on the tomb stones (and therefore made available to us students) about the individual and their relationship to others. My interpretation of the relationships of these families and individuals was to be represented patriarchally - every relationship was based on the relationship to the father or husband. 
Like discussed in the reading Connecting the Dots, the activity we did in class forced us to pick one relationship from a diverse network of other possible relationships to pull data from. Echoed in The Social Network Analysis in Archaeology, Mills states: "The ties may represent different kinds of social relations, ranging from economic transactions to shared identities and other affiliations". In the application of archaeological networks there is an emphasis on nonlinear systems, but the challenge/gap always remains the interpretation of this nonlinear system. 
Social network analysis, when applied properly can reveal alot about the way actors in a social structured network interacts among others. The usefulness to archaeology comes when we can see the connections and ties that originally define relationships, change over time. Archaeology adds a depth of time to this data, however it is not exempt from our own biases when looking into the past and creating conclusions about social networks.
As Mills discusses, there is an issue in SNA with the incorporation of multiple social networks into the analysis. When doing our own social network analysis, we may have observed several different relationship types but we are only able to visualize one at a time. 
The unique time depth perspective that archaeology brings is also explored in the paper by Romanowska et al. 
A quote from this paper that I find resonates with my exploration of this topic: 
"Some models can be built using observations derived from experiments or from systems that can be directly studied. For example, a model of a preindustrial village may assume that people who lived in a single household were in some way related because of what we know from the observations of modern human groups and their family-forming behaviors". The paper notes the shift in the focus from the "detail" to the "whole", which I saw when doing our class activity. Coupling this with the rapid increase of computer power available, makes depicting complicated scenes of relationships much easier to visualize. I do have to credit computers there, I would not be able to recreate the relationships of Roman society by hand on pen and paper. I would 1000% be using the computer for that because omg theres no way I could cleanly depict that manually by hand. 

So while there is the issue of interpretations being one of the most challenging parts of building an ASN, but if we can properly utilize computers for this task (while being reflexive of their history and limitations in this task), we can use this tool for visualizing and understanding complex ancient social systems.
